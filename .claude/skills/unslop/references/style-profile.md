# Author style profile — Johannes Rabauer

## 1. Typography

- Em dashes: not observed in samples; skill default (avoid) stands regardless.
- Quotation marks: straight, consistent.
- Headings: short, mostly 1-3 words, sentence case with occasional capitalized technical terms ("Enter EclipseStore", "Usage", "Conclusion", "Getting Started"). Not full Title Case on every word.
- Lists and bold: genuinely likes numbered lists for technical steps (setup steps, "Decoding the Components", "Value Proposition") and short bulleted Pro/Con lists for tradeoffs. Uses **bold** on the phrase that carries the actual point of a sentence ("**No costs if there is no demand.**", "**fixed monthly costs regardless of its usage**"), not on random keywords. Bold is load-bearing here, not decorative — keep it, but only on the sentence's real payoff.
- Paragraphs: short, especially in the punchier blog-style piece (2-4 sentences); longer, more tutorial-paced paragraphs when walking through a case study or a CLI sequence.
- Other: no emoji observed in his own written blog prose (emoji in the brand-post drafts came from this skill's own suggestions, not from him — treat emoji as optional/light-touch, not a habit to preserve).

## 2. Vocabulary

- Words and connectives he actually uses: "pretty cool", "really great", "really kind and helpful", "a lot", "so many", "best of both worlds", "drop in replacement", "rethink". Plain, warm intensifiers rather than corporate enthusiasm words.
- Words he avoids: inflated marketing language ("game-changer", "revolutionize", "cutting-edge") did not appear anywhere in the samples, technical or spoken. Skip these even though the genre (brand posts) might tempt it.
- Professional jargon and its spelling: "Spring Data", "EclipseStore", "Code Engine", technology names always spelled exactly as the vendor spells them (case-sensitive), not casually respelled.
- Addressing the reader: uses "you"/"your" directly and often, especially when explaining a technical consequence ("your objects", "your application").
- Spelling variety: American English (e.g. "minimize", "optimized").
- Non-native-speaker register (explicitly named as a fact, not a flaw, by the author himself): sentences run more direct and less ornamented than a native speaker might default to. Shorter clauses, fewer idioms, plainer connectors. This is a real trait of his own voice, not a defect to "fix" by inserting native-sounding filler.

## 3. Rhythm

- Typical sentence length: short to medium, plain subject-verb-object order. Not clipped enough to feel curt, but no long subordinate-clause stacking either.
- How he opens texts: with the practical situation or the cost problem directly ("Every Spring project needs storage at some point... and the default gets expensive"), not with a scene-setting hook or a rhetorical question.
- How he closes: with the concrete payoff restated plainly (money saved, a CTA link to try it), sometimes with a co-author credit. Not a moralizing wrap-up.
- Transitions between paragraphs: plain, functional ("So", "Now", numbered step transitions) rather than "Furthermore"/"Moreover".

## 4. Personal habits (tics that must not be cleaned out)

- Concrete numbers over vague claims, always: exact dollar figures, exact row/entry counts, exact percentages ("over 95%", "5,000,000 entries", "30s"). When he doesn't have a real number, he says so rather than inventing one — this profile inherits that as a hard preference, on top of the skill's own Epistemics rule.
- Self-aware, matter-of-fact honesty about his own limits or tradeoffs (non-native English, a tool's Con list) stated plainly and without apologizing more than once.
- Warmth expressed through simple repeated words ("really", "a lot", "so many") rather than varied, fancier synonyms — don't synonym-cycle this away.
- Gives credit by name where due (co-author credits, community/organizer shoutouts).
- Recommends things directly in first person ("I'd like to recommend...") rather than a passive "it is recommended".

## 5. Genres and registers

- Technical blog post / case study: numbered lists for components and setup steps, real code/CLI snippets left verbatim, a Pro/Con list for honest tradeoffs, cost math as the actual argument (not adjectives).
- Spoken/conversational (from rambling sample): warm, direct, short sentences, plain vocabulary, comfortable naming his own non-native English as a simple fact. Community-minded: talks about JUGs (Java User Groups) as something he genuinely values, not just an obligation ("I like those a lot", "they do something for the community").
- Social/brand posts (the genre these profiles will mostly be used for): should borrow the blog voice's directness and concrete-number habit, and the spoken sample's warmth, rather than either the blog's heavier technical-list formatting or excessive spoken-style filler. No em dashes regardless of genre (skill hard rule).

## 6. Explicit rules

- "This is me talking and not really writing" — the rambling sample is speech, weighted for vocabulary/warmth/rhythm signal, not for sentence-level grammar to imitate literally.
- Non-native English phrasing is a real, permanent trait of his voice, not something to smooth into native-sounding idiom.
- No clever reveal phrasing ("X turned out to be the boring part", "the real work sits one level up"). He flagged exactly this as "AI for sure, does not sound like me" (2026-10-13). When a text speaks for him, take the judgment words from what he actually said ("not really the great thing", "the more interesting thing", "pretty interesting") instead of punchier ones he never used.
